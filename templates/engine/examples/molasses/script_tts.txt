In nineteen nineteen, a wave of syrup outran people down a city street.
It's January in Boston, and by the harbor stands a steel tank five stories tall, holding over two million gallons of molasses.
The tank leaks so badly that neighbors bring buckets to collect the drips.
The company's fix? Paint the tank brown, so the leaks don't show.
Two days ago, fresh warm molasses was pumped in on top, and now the walls groan every time it settles.
Then, on an oddly mild afternoon, the rivets start popping like gunshots, and the whole tank splits open.
A brown wave twenty-five feet tall rolls down the street at thirty-five miles an hour.
It throws a truck into the harbor and shoves a firehouse off its foundation.
Twenty-one people never made it out.
But wait. How does molasses move that fast?
You see, molasses is all about temperature.
Ever poured honey straight from the fridge? It barely moves. Warm it up, and it runs like juice.
The warm molasses raced out of the tank, then the freezing evening air thickened it, and it held on to everyone it caught.
After years in court, the rules changed: engineers now have to show their math and sign their plans before anything gets built.
And locals say that on hot summer days, the old waterfront still smells sweet.
