## Cold open

- **N01** Three strokes of a small brass bell.
- **N02** High in the crow's nest, lookout Frederick Fleet grabs the telephone to the bridge.
- **F1** [bm_daniel] "Iceberg right ahead." — *real words, as Fleet recalled them (inquiries)*
- **O1** [am_liam] "Thank you." — *real words, as Fleet recalled them (inquiries)*
- **N03** The most famous warning in history. And the answer was two polite words.
- **N04** For most of the next hour, many people on board had no idea how serious it was. This is that hour: from the iceberg to the first lifeboat.

## Part 1: Less than a minute

- **N05** Let's rewind a few seconds. Sunday night, the fourteenth of April, 1912. A little before eleven forty.
- **N06** Titanic is doing about twenty-two knots. The sea is flat calm, and there is no moon.
- **N07** Up in the crow's nest, Fleet and Reginald Lee have no binoculars. they see a black object, right ahead, high above the water.
- **N08** At first, he said, it looked about as large as two tables put together. He strikes the bell three times, and rings the bridge.
- **N09** Sixth Officer James Moody answers, and passes it straight to the officer in charge, William Murdoch.
- **N10** In the film, the lookouts are distracted, watching two young lovers on the deck below. In the real record, Fleet said he struck the bell as soon as he saw it.
- **N11** Murdoch gives the order.
- **M1** [am_onyx] "Hard-a-starboard!" — *real order, as Boxhall recalled it*
- **N12** You see, that sounds like: turn right. But in 1912, steering orders were still given as if the helmsman held an old tiller. Push the tiller to starboard, and the bow swings the other way. To port. To the left.
- **N13** Murdoch also rings the engine room. Quartermaster Robert Hichens spins the wheel, hard over.
- **N14** How long do they have? You'll often hear: thirty-seven seconds. That number comes from a turning test on her sister ship, Olympic, not from that night. The honest answer is: less than a minute.
- **N15** Slowly, the bow swings left. About two compass points. Almost enough.
- **N16** The iceberg scrapes along the right side of the bow.
- **N17** At the back of the ship, Quartermaster George Rowe felt a slight jar, and looked at his watch. Twenty minutes to twelve.
- **N18** On the bridge, Murdoch pulls the lever that closes the watertight doors deep below. Captain Smith runs in.
- **S1** [bm_george] "Close the watertight door." — *as Hichens recalled it (British inquiry)*
- **M2** [am_onyx] "They are already closed." — *as Hichens recalled it (British inquiry)*

## Part 2: Ice for this

- **N19** Down in the cabins, most people feel almost nothing. Lawrence Beesley, a science teacher in second class, felt what he called nothing more than an extra heave of the engines.
- **N20** Then the engines stop. And steam starts roaring out of the funnels, so loud that people can hardly talk on deck.
- **N21** On the forward well deck, the iceberg has left behind a couple of tons of ice. In the smoking room, a card player points to his glass of whisky.
- **G1** [am_michael] "Just run along the deck and see if any ice has come aboard. I would like some for this." — *as Beesley recalled it (1912)*
- **N22** Some passengers picked up the ice, and a few played with it.
- **N23** Deep in the bow, the five postal clerks had reportedly been celebrating. Tomorrow was their colleague Oscar Woody's birthday.
- **N24** Now seawater is rising in the mail room, and they start hauling sacks of registered mail up to higher decks.

## Part 3: Six compartments

- **N25** Fourth Officer Joseph Boxhall goes down to look. At first, he sees no damage at all, and reports back. Captain Smith sends him to find the carpenter. The carpenter meets him on the stairs.
- **K1** [am_adam] "The ship is making water." — *as Boxhall recalled it (US inquiry)*
- **N26** Then a mail clerk. Boxhall goes to see for himself. Mail bags are floating in the hold.
- **J1** [am_eric] "The mail hold is filling rapidly." — *as Boxhall recalled it (US inquiry)*
- **N27** In boiler room number six, about ten minutes after the impact, the water is already eight feet deep. The firemen climb out.
- **N28** Captain Smith sends for the one man who knows this ship better than anyone: her designer, Thomas Andrews.
- **N29** Together they go down through the front of the ship. Reports come in: the mail room, and even the squash court, are flooding.
- **N30** Here's what we know now. The iceberg didn't cut a giant gash. Shipbuilder Edward Wilding later estimated the openings added up to about twelve square feet. Roughly the size of a small door, spread out in narrow cracks along the bow.
- **N31** You see, Titanic's hull is divided by steel walls into watertight compartments. She could stay afloat with up to four of her front compartments flooded.
- **N32** The iceberg opened six.
- **N33** And those walls didn't go all the way up. Think of an ice cube tray. Tip one end down, and the water fills the first cell, spills over the top into the next, then the next.
- **N34** Titanic was going to sink. The only question was when.
- **N35** In the film, Andrews calls it a mathematical certainty, and gives her an hour or two. In real life, nobody recorded his exact words. What we have comes from his biographer, writing later that year: she could not live more than an hour and a half.
- **N36** It's about midnight.

## Part 4: CQD

- **N37** Captain Smith orders the lifeboats uncovered. Then he goes to the wireless room, where Jack Phillips has just finished a long batch of messages for Cape Race.
- **N38** Soon after midnight, Phillips starts tapping out the distress call of the day: C Q D.
- **N39** Boxhall works out the ship's position, and they send it to every ship in range. Forty-one forty-six north. Fifty fourteen west.
- **N40** But that position was wrong. When the wreck was found in 1985, it lay about thirteen nautical miles to the east.
- **N41** Later, Harold Bride said he joked to Phillips about the new distress call.
- **B1** [bm_lewis] "Send S O S. It's the new call, and besides, this may be your last chance to send it!" — *Bride's own account, 1912*
- **N42** S O S wasn't brand new. Ships had used it since 1909. But on British ships it was still rare. Phillips sends both, and keeps sending, for the rest of the night.
- **N43** Fifty-eight miles away, on the small Cunard liner Carpathia, wireless operator Harold Cottam, close to bedtime, calls Titanic with some routine messages. The answer, as he remembered it:
- **T1** [bm_fable] "Come at once. We have struck a berg. It is a C Q D." — *message as Cottam recalled it (US inquiry)*
- **N44** Cottam runs to the bridge, and Captain Arthur Rostron is woken. Carpathia turns around and races north-west, toward the ice.

## Part 5: The first boat

- **N45** Up on the boat deck, around twelve twenty-five, the order comes: women and children into the boats.
- **N46** Stewards have been waking people and handing out lifebelts. But it's freezing, the steam is roaring, and the ship still feels steady under their feet. There has been no boat drill. Why leave the biggest ship in the world for a little wooden boat in the dark?
- **N47** Around twelve forty-five, the first lifeboat, number seven, is lowered. It can carry sixty-five people. Fewer than thirty are in it, men among them. Nobody is sure the boats can be lowered full.
- **N48** It started with three strokes of a bell, and a polite thank you. An hour later, it was still quiet. A half-empty boat, touching black water, under a ship with all her lights still on.
- **N49** And if Andrews' hour and a half was right, about half of it was already gone.
