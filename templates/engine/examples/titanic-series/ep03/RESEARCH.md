# Ep03 "The iceberg": research (11:39 p.m. to ~12:45 a.m., 14-15 April 1912)

All clock times are **Titanic ship's time** (about 1 h 33 min to 2 h ahead of New York time; the exact offset is debated). Witnesses read different clocks and watches, so every time is "about".
Rule used: a fact goes into the script only with 2+ independent sources. A quote is used word for word and labelled "as X recalled at the US/British inquiry".

## Mechanism notes ("You see," cut-aways)

**1. The helm order (tiller language).** In 1912 British ships gave helm orders as if moving a tiller. "Hard-a-starboard" = push the tiller to starboard, so the rudder and the bow swing to **port** (left). Modern "starboard" orders mean the opposite; the change came in the 1930s (29). Visual: a chibi hand pushes a tiller right, bow swings left. The ship's head swung about two points (22.5°) before she struck (3, 6).

**2. The 37 seconds.** Not measured on the night. It is the time Olympic took, in a trial at about 21.5 knots, to turn two points after the helm was put hard over (Wilding, Q25292, 12); the British Report used it and estimated the berg was seen at about 500 yards (6). Several witnesses describe the order and impact as almost simultaneous (19), and the time from sighting to the helm order is unknown. Safe: "Less than a minute, perhaps around half a minute."

**3. The damage.** Not a 300-ft gash. The ice opened a series of seams and punctures along the starboard bow, from the forepeak to the forward bunker of No. 5 boiler room; Wilding estimated the openings totalled "about 12 square feet", averaging "about three-quarters of an inch" wide (11, 21). Length: often given as about 300 ft (18; popular books); Maltin puts it nearer 200-250 ft (21). Safe: "a string of narrow openings spread along the first part of the hull, about the size of a door in total".
Compartments breached: forepeak, holds 1, 2, 3, boiler room 6 (five fully open), plus a small leak in boiler room 5's bunker (7, 8, 11) = **six**.

**4. Why four was survivable and six was not.** She could float with any two compartments flooded, and could survive with the first three or four at the bow open (8, 11, 18). With the forepeak, holds 1-3 and boiler room 6 all flooding, the bow sank low enough that water would rise above the top of the bulkhead between boiler rooms 6 and 5 (its top was at E deck in most accounts; not checked in a primary source this session, so don't name the deck on screen) and spill aft, like an ice-cube tray tilted at one end: each cell fills and overflows into the next (8, 9). The Report: "she could not remain afloat with the four forward compartments and the forward boiler room (No. 6) also flooded" (8). Wilding: "any three of these forward compartments" (11). Safe: "Titanic could stay afloat with up to four of her front compartments flooded. The iceberg opened six."

**5. The doors.** Murdoch's lever closed the watertight doors in the engine and boiler rooms (4, 6). The bulkheads were walls with open tops, not sealed boxes: this is the key to the tray image (9).

**6. Wrong position.** Boxhall's position (41°46'N, 50°14'W) was about 13 nautical miles west of where the wreck was found in 1985 (41°43.9'N, 49°56.8'W) (22, 23). Halpern's explanation: a timing error in converting star-sight times (22). Carpathia, steaming toward the CQD position, still met the lifeboats (22). An earlier position (50°24'W, from dead reckoning) was sent first; coordinates of that first call differ between sources (22, 23): don't show it.

## Small-object hooks (paradox opening), ranked by documentation

1. **The bell rung three times** (1, 2, 3): strongest. "Three strokes of a small bell, and the largest ship in the world had 160 minutes left." (2 h 40 min sinking: 6.)
2. **The telephone and "Thank you."** (1, 2, 3): a polite two-word reply. Paradox: "The most famous warning in history was answered with good manners."
3. **Ice for a whisky glass** (17): Beesley's smoking-room passenger asked for ice "for this" while the forward deck was covered. Plus Lucas's "couple of tons" (13) and Candee's crewman with ice in his hands (37).
4. **Mail sacks**: 3,364 mailbags aboard; five sea-post clerks (3 American, 2 British) were celebrating Oscar Woody's 44th birthday when the ship struck, then hauled registered mail upward as the hold flooded; none survived (26, 27, 5, 18).
5. **A pocket watch**: clerk John Starr March's watch, recovered with his body, stopped at 1:27 (Smithsonian National Postal Museum) (26, 27). Good hook, but the time is outside this episode: use as a teaser/end callback only.
6. **Rowe's watch**: "looked at my watch ... 20 minutes to 12" (14).
**Avoid**: "a glass of water rippling": no source found; it is a film-style image (looks like Jurassic Park). If used, label clearly as illustration.

## Film vs real (1997 *Titanic*)

**Card A: The lookouts.**
- Film: just before the sighting, the lookouts are watching (and amused by) Jack and Rose on the well deck below, then see the berg dead ahead (34, 35). Murdoch also glances at the couple (33).
- Real: Fleet said he struck the bell "as soon as I saw it" (2, Q17280) and testified to no distraction. The real problems: no binoculars in the crow's nest, a flat calm, moonless night (ep02 research). Safe line: "In the film, the lookouts are distracted by two lovers. In the real record, Fleet says he rang the bell the moment he saw it."

**Card B: Andrews' "mathematical certainty".**
- Film: Andrews tells Ismay, "She's made of iron, sir. I assure you she can, and she will. It is a mathematical certainty." (36) and gives one to two hours (study-guide summaries; check the exact film line before quoting it).
- Real: no survivor heard Andrews' words. His 1912 biographer wrote that he reported the ship "could not live more than an hour and a half" (24), and others say about two hours (25, 18). She floated about 2 h 40 min (6). Safe line: "Nobody recorded Andrews' exact words. What we have is a biographer's report: an hour and a half, maybe two."

**Option C (the film got it right):** Murdoch's sequence in the film (telegraph to stop / full astern, "Is it hard over?", watertight lever, hard-a-port after impact) closely follows inquiry testimony (33; compare 4, 6). And ice on deck is real (13, 17, 37). (The film's portrayal of Murdoch later in the night led Fox to apologise to his home town (38): not this episode.)

## Disputed / avoid

- **"37 seconds"**: from Olympic's trial, not the night (12, 6); testimony suggests the gap may have been shorter (19). Say "less than a minute".
- **"Full speed astern"**: Boxhall saw the telegraphs at full astern (4) and the Report accepts "Stop. Full speed astern" (6), but stokers (Barrett, Beauchamp) and greaser Scott describe "Stop" and timings that do not fit a full reversal before impact (19). Safe: "Murdoch rang the engine room" / "ordered the engines stopped and reversed" (as Boxhall recalled). Don't claim reversing caused the collision.
- **The "port-around"** (hard-a-port after the first turn to swing the stern clear): based on Murdoch's words as Boxhall recalled (4) and QM Olliver; whether it was carried out is debated. Mention only as "he tried to swing her stern clear", attributed.
- **Louise Patten's "steering error" (2010)**: in her novel *Good as Gold*, she said her grandfather Lightoller told the family that Hichens panicked and turned the wrong way. Doubts: Hichens, Boxhall and the Report agree the order was hard-a-starboard and it was carried out (3, 4, 6); Moody checked the helm (29); Hichens was experienced; second-hand family story first published 98 years later in a novel (29, 30). Safe: "A later family story claimed a steering mistake; the inquiry witnesses all describe the order being carried out." Or skip.
- **300-ft gash**: myth (21). Use "openings", "about 12 square feet".
- **Andrews' exact words and time estimate**: secondhand (24, 25). No "mathematical certainty" outside the film.
- **"Iceberg right ahead!" as a shout**: it was said on the telephone (1, 2). Fleet's exact punctuation is unknown.
- **"It's a CQD, old man"**: not in Cottam's US testimony (15); avoid the "old man".
- **"First SOS ever"**: false; SOS had been used since at least 1909 (28). Say "one of the first ships to use the new SOS".
- **Time of first CQD**: 12:05 order (Ballard via 18) vs 12:15 first CQD (10). Say "soon after midnight".
- **Rowe saw a boat at "25 minutes after 12"** (14): conflicts with ~12:40 for the first launch (31, 32): different clocks. Use "around 12:45" for boat 7.
- **Boat 7 occupancy**: ~28 (18); estimates vary (27-28): say "fewer than thirty people".
- **Passengers playing football with ice**: secondary only (Bartlett via 18; Lord). Primary: ice on the well deck (13), ice for a drink (17). Safe: "Some passengers picked up the ice, and a few played with it."
- **Iceberg photo**: unproven which berg.
- **Glass of water rippling**: no source; don't present as fact.
